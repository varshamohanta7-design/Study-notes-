1. What are Lipids? (Definition & Core Concept)

PDF Page 2

• The term LIPID is used to denote a diverse group of organic compounds that are insoluble in water (hydrophobic), but soluble in non-polar organic solvents.

Key Non-Polar Solvents (PDF list):
1. Chloroform (CHCl3) 2. Carbon Disulphide (CS2) 3. Benzene (C6H6) 4. Ether (C4H10O)

★ Key Physical & Chemical Properties of Lipids

  • Organic Composition: Formed mainly of fats and oils.
  • Hydrophobic Nature: Repel water molecules and do not dissolve in aqueous environments.
  • Concentrated Energy Storage: Fats & oils in the form of Triglycerides are highly efficient energy storage molecules, providing concentrated calories upon metabolic oxidation.
  • Cell Membrane Integrity: Phospholipids are essential building blocks of cell membranes; they regulate selective permeability.
  • Cellular Signaling & Fluidity: Lipids such as cholesterol and steroid hormones possess a 4-ring structure that maintains membrane fluidity and aids in intercellular signaling.
  • Nutritional Significance: Supply essential fatty acids (which the body cannot synthesize on its own) and facilitate the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

Exam Tip: Lipids Water Insolubility

Remember for competitive exams (ICAR JRF / NET): Lipids are NOT defined by a single repeating monomer unit (unlike proteins or starch). They are classified based on physical solubility: Insoluble in H2O, soluble in organic solvents!

3. Detailed Study: Simple Lipids & Fatty Acids

PDF Page 6-11

3.1 Simple Lipids (Triglycerides & Waxes)

• Esters of fatty acids with various alcohols. Yield glycerol + fatty acids upon complete hydrolysis.

1. Triglycerides (Triacylglycerol)
  • Formed by 1 Glycerol backbone linked to 3 Fatty Acid chains via Ester Bonds.
  • Stored in adipose tissue (body fat) as long-term energy reserves.
  • Fats vs Oils Distinction:
    - Fats: Solid at room temperature (20°C).
    - Oils: Liquid at room temperature (20°C).
1) FATTY ACID 2 (R2) FATTY ACID 3 (R3) Triglyceride Ester Structure
2. Waxes
  • Esters of long-chain fatty acids with long-chain monohydric alcohols.
  • Found protective on surfaces of plant leaves, fruits, and animal tissues.
  • Examples (from PDF):
    • Animal Wax: Beeswax (in honeycomb), Earwax (cerumen).
    • Plant Wax: Carnauba wax (from palm leaves).
💡 Key Fact: Wax vs Triglyceride Triglycerides contain glycerol (trihydric alcohol), whereas Waxes contain a single long-chain alcohol (monohydric)!

3.2 Fatty Acids Structure & Saturated vs. Unsaturated

• Fatty acids consist of long hydrocarbon chains with a Methyl group (-CH3) at one end and a Carboxylic acid group (-COOH) at the other end.
• Most naturally occurring fatty acids contain an EVEN number of carbon atoms in the chain.

Parameter Saturated Fatty Acids Unsaturated Fatty Acids
Carbon-Carbon Bonds Only SINGLE bonds (C-C) Contains DOUBLE bonds (C=C)
Hydrogen Capacity Fully saturated with hydrogen atoms Missing hydrogen atoms due to C=C
Molecular Shape Linear / Straight chain (packs tightly) Kinked / Bent chain at double bond
State at Room Temp Generally SOLID Generally LIQUID
PDF Examples Butyric acid, Caproic acid, Lauric acid, Stearic acid, Palmitic acid Oleic acid, Linoleic acid, Linolenic acid, Arachidonic acid
A. Sub-classification of Unsaturated FA
  • Monounsaturated (MUFA): Contains ONE double bond.
    • Example (PDF): Oleic Acid
  • Polyunsaturated (PUFA): Contains MORE THAN ONE double bond.
    • Examples (PDF): Linoleic acid, α-Linolenic acid, Arachidonic acid
Exact Formulas from PDF:
α-Linolenic acid:
CH3CH2CH=CHCH2CH=CHCH2CH=CH(CH2)7COOH
Arachidonic acid:
CH3(CH2)4CH=CHCH2CH=CHCH2CH=CHCH2CH=CH(CH2)3COOH
B. Cis vs. Trans Isomerism
  • Cis Configuration: Hydrogen atoms are on the SAME SIDE of the double bond.
    • All naturally occurring food oils exist in CIS form.
  • Trans Configuration: Hydrogen atoms are on OPPOSITE SIDES across the double bond.
⚠️ Critical Process (PDF Page 11): PARTIAL HYDROGENATION of edible oils converts natural cis double bonds into trans configuration!

PAGE 04 • COMPOUND & DERIVED

4. Compound Lipids & Derived Lipids

PDF Page 12-13

4.1 Compound (Complex) Lipids

• Esters of fatty acids containing non-lipid chemical groups (such as phosphorus, carbohydrate, or protein) in addition to alcohol and fatty acids.

1. Phospholipids

• Consists of 2 Fatty Acids esterified to Glycerol. In place of the 3rd fatty acid, there is a Phosphoric acid group and a nitrogen-containing base.
• Key Example (PDF): Lecithin (found abundantly in egg yolk).

2. Glycolipids

• Contains Fatty acid + Carbohydrate component + Nitrogenous base.
• Note: Generally NOT a major component of food lipids.
• PDF Examples: Cerebrosides and Gangliosides.

3. Lipoproteins

• Macromolecular complexes of lipids bound with specific proteins. Found circulating in mammalian plasma.
• PDF Plasma Lipoprotein Classes:

1. Chylomicrons 2. VLDL (Very Low Density) 3. LDL (Low Density) 4. HDL (High Density)

4.2 Derived Lipids

• Produced when simple and compound lipids undergo hydrolysis. Includes lipid derivatives and lipid-like bioactive substances:

A. Carotenes

• Plant accessory pigments (e.g., in carrots).
• In animal tissues, converted to Vitamin A, which synthesizes the visual pigment Rhodopsin.

B. Sterols

• Amphipathic lipids containing both polar and non-polar groups.
• PDF Examples: Cholesterol (animals), Ergosterol (fungi/plants).

C. Fat-Soluble Vitamins

• Critical micronutrients absorbed along with dietary fats:

Vit A Vit D Vit E Vit K

Common Mistake in Exams ❌

Don't confuse Lecithin with Cephalin! Both are phospholipids, but Lecithin contains Choline as its nitrogenous base (egg yolk), whereas Cephalin contains Ethanolamine or Serine.

5. Topper's Quick Exam Revision Cheat Sheet

10-Min Flash Revision

⚡ Rapid Memory Points

  • Definition: Water insoluble, soluble in CHCl3, CS2, benzene, ether.
  • Simple Lipids: Triglycerides (Glycerol + 3 FA) & Waxes (Long FA + Long Alcohol).
  • Saturated FA: Only single C-C bonds (Palmitic, Stearic, Caproic, Lauric, Butyric).
  • Oleic Acid: Classic Monounsaturated Fatty Acid (MUFA).
  • Natural Food Oils: Always exist in CIS configuration.

⚡ Key Formulas & Examples

  • Partial Hydrogenation: Converts cis → trans fatty acids.
  • Phospholipid Example: Lecithin (found in egg yolk).
  • Glycolipids: Cerebrosides & Gangliosides.
  • Plasma Lipoproteins: Chylomicron, VLDL, LDL, HDL.
  • Fat Soluble Vitamins: Vit A, D, E, K.
